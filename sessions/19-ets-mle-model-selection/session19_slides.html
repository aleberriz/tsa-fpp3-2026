    ETS classification

    Session 19 · Interpreting ETS models

    The ETS letters specify errors, trend and seasonality.

    ETS(M,Ad,M): relative errors, damped additive trend, multiplicative season

    Read ETS as Error, Trend and Seasonality. A means additive, M multiplicative, N none, and Ad a damped additive trend. The trend and season determine the smoothing method, while the error type describes how unpredictable deviations enter the model.

    The classification notebook collects the state space equations for the methods already encountered. Reading the letters connects a specification with the components it includes; it does not require deriving every cell of that table.

    Multiplicative trends are outside this course's main classification. The familiar no-trend, additive-trend and damped-trend choices already cover the methods developed in the smoothing block.
    From update rule to statistical model

    Session 19 · Interpreting ETS models

    An observation error also changes the estimated level.

    SES with additive errors: yt = ℓt−1 + et ℓt = ℓt−1 + αet

    In simple exponential smoothing (SES), observation yt equals the preceding level ℓt−1 plus an unpredictable error et. The smoothing weight α determines how much of that error revises the level to ℓt. Giving the errors a probability distribution also gives the model forecast uncertainty and a likelihood.

    The observation equation says how a value could be generated from the previous state and a new shock. The state equation says how that shock changes the next estimate. For additive normal-error SES, independent zero-mean normal shocks complete this statistical description.

    This also explains why a point forecast is only part of a model. Different possible future shocks produce different future paths, giving a distribution from which intervals can be obtained.
    Response and innovation residuals

    Session 19 · Interpreting ETS models

    The error model describes deviations in units or proportions.

    Additive innovation: yt − ŷt|t−1 Multiplicative innovation: (yt − ŷt|t−1) / ŷt|t−1
    • Here yt is the observation and ŷt|t−1 its earlier forecast. Their difference is an error in the data's units. Dividing by the forecast gives a relative error, the multiplicative innovation.
    • These different error assumptions give different likelihoods, which score how well the model accounts for the data. Refitting can therefore change both point forecasts and prediction intervals.
    Relative errors can have a stable spread when response-unit errors grow with the level. That is a reason to consider multiplicative errors, but the error and season letters describe different parts of the model.

    The state space equations use the one-step innovation to update the states. Their parameter conventions explain the beta versus beta-star distinction encountered in Session 18 on seasonal smoothing. Different error specifications also change the fitting objective.
    Likelihood and SSE

    Session 19 · Likelihood and model selection

    Likelihood scores the data while fitting changes the parameters.

    • Each distribution, predicted from earlier data, assigns a density (curve height) to the observation. Multiplying these densities gives the likelihood, while adding their logarithms gives the log-likelihood.
    • With independent additive normal errors, maximizing likelihood and minimizing the sum of squared errors (SSE) give the same fitted parameters.
    Likelihood multiplies conditional density heights for the observations. Logs turn that product into a sum without changing its maximizing parameters because the logarithm is strictly increasing.

    A density height is not the probability of one exact continuous value. Making a normal distribution wider spreads its mass more thinly, so uncertainty cannot be increased without affecting the likelihood score. The short preparation guide develops this idea before the fitting example.
    Information criteria

    Session 19 · Likelihood and model selection

    AIC rewards fit but charges for extra parameters.

    AIC = −2 log L + 2k One extra parameter costs 2 AIC units.
    • AIC is Akaike's information criterion. L is the fitted likelihood and k counts estimated parameters, including free initial states and the error variance. Better fit raises log L and lowers the first term.
    • Each extra parameter adds two to the penalty, so it must improve fit enough to reduce total AIC. Prefer the smaller total when the likelihoods are comparable.
    The information-criterion count includes the error variance as well as smoothing parameters and free initial states. It therefore differs from counts used for some residual-variance corrections.

    AICc adds a finite-sample correction to AIC; BIC uses a different penalty based on sample size. These are supporting details in the notebook. An increase in parameter count alone does not order likelihoods across arbitrary, nonnested ETS specifications.

    Session 19 · Likelihood and model selection

    AICc needs comparable likelihoods to rank models.

    • AICc is AIC with a correction for small samples. It acts as a referee for ETS candidates fitted to the same observations and response scale using compatible likelihood definitions. A tiny gap provides little evidence to separate them.
    • For a model of a response y and another of log(y), compare forecasts on a common scale using rolling-origin evaluation. This repeatedly trains on the past and tests later observations at the horizon the decision requires.
    Model-family labels alone do not establish likelihood comparability. The observations, response scale and likelihood definitions must agree, including any constants and conditioning conventions relevant to the comparison.

    A criterion computed for log(y) is not directly comparable to one for y without accounting for the transformation. Rolling-origin errors on a common response scale offer a practical alternative, aligned to the decision's horizon.
    Australian holiday-trips dataset: model selection

    Session 19 · Australian holiday-trips dataset

    Automatic ETS selection still leaves a model to interpret.

    The aus_holidays dataset records Australian domestic holiday trips by quarter, with Trips measured in millions. ETS(Trips) selects an applicable exponential smoothing model.

    fit <- aus_holidays |> model(auto = ETS(Trips)) report(fit) tidy(fit) glance(fit) components(fit) |> autoplot()

    report() names the model, tidy() gives parameters, and glance() reports selection criteria. The components plot shows how the included level, trend and seasonal estimates evolve.

    Fixing part of an ETS specification restricts the candidate set. An automatic winner is therefore the best eligible fit under the selected criterion, not a guarantee that every plausible model was considered.

    Changing measurement units should preserve theoretical AIC differences. If an almost tied numerical ranking flips after rescaling, inspect optimization and fit stability; the units have not created a new theoretical selection rule.
    Check the selected model

    Session 19 · Australian holiday-trips dataset

    Residual structure can reveal a weakness in the selected model.

    fit |> gg_tsresiduals() augment(fit) |> features(.innov, ljung_box, lag = 8)

    Here fit is the selected ETS model for the quarterly Australian holiday-trips dataset. The .innov column contains its innovation residuals, the fitted errors on the model's assumed scale. Plotting them and testing their autocorrelation checks whether predictable patterns remain, even in the model preferred by AICc, the corrected information criterion.

    gg_tsresiduals() uses innovation residuals for these model diagnostics. The distinction matters when calculating statistics yourself from augment(): response residuals and innovations are on different scales under multiplicative errors.

    Session 9 on residual diagnostics supplies the interpretation. A nonsignificant autocorrelation test does not certify every model assumption, and a small AICc does not replace checking correlation, spread and tails.
    Forecasting with ETS

    Session 19 · Forecasts and uncertainty

    A forecast interval expresses uncertainty at a stated horizon.

    fit |> forecast(h = 8) |> autoplot(aus_holidays) fit |> forecast(h = 8) |> hilo(level = 95)
    • Here fit models the quarterly Australian holiday-trips dataset, aus_holidays. The horizon h = 8 asks for eight quarters ahead. .mean contains the forecast-distribution mean, and hilo(level = 95) returns its 95% interval.
    • Use the path and intervals together when assessing a decision. Their uncertainty depends on the fitted assumptions, and some ETS models obtain the intervals through simulation.
    Before using an interval, examine whether the innovations support the model's assumptions about correlation and variation. Even the preferred candidate can leave predictable structure behind.

    The .mean column is the forecast-distribution mean. For nonlinear seasonal models it need not match the traditional recursion with future errors set to zero. Simulated normal shocks and resampled residuals are different ways to generate future paths; their assumptions should not be conflated.
    Cross-validation and AIC practice

    Session 19 · ETS homework and review

    Homework: test model selection using only information then available.

    • Complete the holiday-trip exercises in 19_B and the two-model AIC comparison, where Akaike's information criterion balances fit and complexity.
    • For the monthly corticosteroid-cost dataset, repeat automatic selection within every cross-validation training set. Use origins with twelve observed future months and pool errors across horizons 1 to 12.

    Selecting a model once on the full dataset would let earlier training sets use information from their future, making that evaluation unreliable.

    Automatic selection is part of the forecasting procedure being evaluated. Every training fold must choose its model using only that fold's observations, even if the selected structure changes over time.

    For the monthly corticosteroid-cost exercise, retain origins with all twelve future months observed. Pooling errors across horizons 1 to 12 evaluates that full forecast window; it is not a collection of twelve-step-ahead errors alone.

    Session 19 · ETS homework and review

    Each calculation should support a forecasting decision.

    • For an exponential smoothing model, explain which estimates were available before an observation arrived and which changed afterward. For a seasonal forecast, identify the index belonging to its target date.
    • Use that reasoning to check your arithmetic, then judge the forecasts with held-out errors and residual diagnostics. Explain the assumptions behind their uncertainty and why the chosen horizon fits the business decision.
    The earlier toolbox still applies to every model in this block: transformations define the response scale, intervals describe uncertainty, and held-out evaluation tests a forecast at its intended horizon. A calculation is most useful when its interpretation is stated alongside it.
