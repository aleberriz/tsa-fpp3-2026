    19_A › General notation

    fpp3 8.5  ·  the letter that changes the intervals

    The error letter leaves the forecast alone and reshapes the interval.

    • Additive error: the forecast misses by about the same amount whatever the level. Think of a thermometer that is off by two degrees in winter and summer alike.
    • Multiplicative error: it misses by about the same percentage. A sales forecast that is off by 5% behaves like this, since 5% is more units in a busy month.
    • The point forecasts are the same for both, given the same parameters. The intervals are not, and neither is the likelihood, which is how the data choose between them.

    Nine methods with two error types each give eighteen models, and the referee chooses among them.

    This is why Session 18 paired a multiplicative season with multiplicative errors: when the swings grow with the level, the misses usually do too.

    Behind the scenes fable writes each model in state space form, where every equation is updated by the one-step error. That is also why its beta is αβ*, the point from Session 18.
    19_B › Minimizing the SSE vs maximizing the likelihood

    fpp3 8.6  ·  the reading: 19_A_MLE_Informal_Intro.pdf

    Fit by likelihood: make the data you saw most probable.

    The reading derives it; the final asks for the idea, not the derivation.

    Why logs: the likelihood is a product of many small densities, which is awkward to compute and to maximize. Its log is a sum, and because the log is strictly increasing, the parameters that maximize one maximize the other. The reading shows that for normal data the maximum-likelihood estimates of μ and σ are the sample mean and the uncorrected sample standard deviation.
    19_B › AIC

    fpp3 8.6  ·  AIC, AICc, BIC

    AIC is the fit, plus a charge for every number the model had to estimate.

    AIC = −2 log(L) + 2k k: parameters and initial states, residual variance included AICc = AIC + 2k(k + 1) / (T − k − 1) corrects for short series; what ETS() uses BIC = AIC + k [log(T) − 2] charges more per parameter once T ≥ 8
    • More parameters always fit the training data better, so the first term alone would always choose the biggest model. The charge stops that by putting a price on Session 18's elephant.
    • Lower is better, for all three. The numbers mean nothing on their own; only differences between models fitted to the same data do.
    • By hand: China's population model has 4 parameters and initial states plus the residual variance, so k = 5, and AIC = −2 × log L + 10. The notebook checks it against glance().

    Given two log-likelihoods and two k, choosing by AIC is two multiplications and a comparison.

    The inherited notebook defined k without the residual variance; fpp3 8.6 includes it, and the notebook's own China example used k = 4 + 1. Corrected in this build.

    Why the BIC threshold is T = 8: its extra charge per parameter is log(T) − 2, which is positive once T exceeds e², about 7.4.
    19_B › Information criteria vs cross-validation

    Two referees  ·  when each one is allowed on the pitch

    AICc compares models of one class on one response, and anything else needs cross-validation.

    ComparisonAICc?Cross-validation?
    ETS(A,A,N) vs ETS(A,Ad,N), both on yyes: same class, same responseyes
    ETS on y vs ETS on log(y)no: different responsesyes
    ETS vs seasonal naive, or vs ARIMAno: different classesyes
    • Why the restriction. A likelihood is a probability density of one particular variable under one particular kind of model. Two likelihoods of different variables, or from different kinds of model, are not on the same scale.
    • The trade-off. AICc is free: one fit per model, and it uses the whole series. Cross-validation costs hundreds of fits, but it tests forecasts directly and compares anything with anything.
    • In practice: let AICc choose within ETS, then let cross-validation judge the winner against the benchmarks. Group Assignment 2 is designed this way.

    AICc referees within the ETS family and cross-validation can referee any comparison, so check which kind of comparison you are making.

    ARIMA is the model class of the follow-up course, Forecasting for Time Series; it appears here only as an example of "another class". The same AICc machinery chooses ARIMA orders there, within the ARIMA family.
    19_B › Model combinations that are excluded

    The fine print

    Three combinations are off the menu, and multiplicative errors need positive data.

    • ETS(A,N,M), ETS(A,A,M), ETS(A,Ad,M) are excluded: additive errors with a multiplicative season can divide by values close to zero and blow up. That leaves 15 of the 18 models for ETS() to choose from.
    • Multiplicative errors are only for strictly positive series. With zeros or negative values ETS() does not consider them at all.
    • Zeros in a series that needs a log: Session 11's log1p() is one way around, at the cost of a transformation to undo.

    This is why Session 18 wrote error("M") next to every season("M").

    fpp3 8.6 gives the reason as division by values potentially close to zero in the state equations. You need the list and the reason in one sentence, not the algebra.
    19_B › Residuals and multiplicative errors

    Session 8's two residual columns, one last time

    With multiplicative errors, the residual you diagnose is a percentage.

    .resid yt − ŷt|t−1 in the units of the series .innov (yt − ŷt|t−1) / ŷt|t−1 multiplicative error: a relative miss .innov yt − ŷt|t−1 additive error: the same as .resid
    • In HW09 the two columns differed because of a log. Here they differ with no transformation at all, because the model's error is relative.
    • Run Session 9's diagnostics on .innov. It is the error the model assumes to be white noise; .resid will show a spread that grows with the level even when the model is right.
    • Homework Exercise 2 checks this on the total of Australian holiday trips.

    Diagnose the error the model actually assumes. For multiplicative models that is a percentage miss.

    gg_tsresiduals() uses the innovation residuals by default, so the three-panel plot is already the right one. The difference matters when you compute residual statistics yourself from augment().
    19_B › Example 1: national populations

    Now we compute  ·  let ETS() choose

    Leave the formula empty and ETS() tries all fifteen, keeping the lowest AICc.

    fit <- global_economy |> mutate(Pop = Population / 1e6) |> model(ets = ETS(Pop)) fit |> filter(Country == "China") |> glance() # log_lik, AIC, AICc, BIC -2 * log_lik + 2 * (4 + 1) # 395.77: the AIC, rebuilt by hand aus_holidays |> model(ETS(Trips)) # ETS(M,N,A) # MNA AICc 227.8 MNM AICc 228.3 ANA AICc 230.1
    • One line fits a separate model to each of 263 countries. One country has missing values, which ETS() refuses, so it warns and moves on.
    • China's AIC rebuilt by hand matches glance(): this is slide 6's formula applied to real output.
    • Holiday trips are a near tie. M,N,A and M,N,M are about half a point apart, and measuring the series in thousands instead of millions flips the choice. When the referee cannot separate two models, either is defensible.

    An automatic choice is a recommendation with a margin. Look at the margin before you trust the choice.

    To restrict the search, fix any letter: ETS(y ~ season(c("A", "M"))) searches only seasonal models, which Exercise 1's extra check uses on Brisbane.

    Argentina's population gets ETS(A,A,N) with α and β near 1: a smooth series whose latest level and slope are nearly all you need.
    19_B › Forecasting with ETS models

    fpp3 8.7  ·  the payoff of the block

    The chosen model forecasts with Session 12's intervals. The block ends where it started: a forecast and a range.

    fit |> forecast(h = 8) |> autoplot(aus_holidays) fit |> forecast(h = 8) |> hilo(level = 95) # Quarter .mean 95% # 2018 Q1 12.7 [11.5, 13.9] # 2018 Q2 10.9 [ 9.8, 12.0]
    • Point forecasts are the means of the forecast distributions, except for models with a multiplicative season. This one's season is additive.
    • The intervals depend on the error letter: with multiplicative errors they widen with the level, as slide 4 drew.
    • When there is no formula for the variance, forecast() simulates thousands of future paths and takes their percentiles: Session 12's bootstrap idea, with simulated errors.

    This is the last step of the workflow the block has followed: name the model, fit it, check its residuals, compare it, and forecast with a range.

    Before quoting these intervals, the Session 9 checks still apply: the residuals (.innov) should look like white noise with a stable spread. The chosen model is the best of the family by AICc; it is not automatically a good model.
