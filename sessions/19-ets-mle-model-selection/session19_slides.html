    From Holt-Winters to ETS

    Session 19 · From Holt-Winters to ETS

    Last session we chose the form. Today ETS() chooses it, fits it and forecasts.

    fit_open <- aus_holidays |>
  model(
    additive = ETS(Trips ~ error("A") + trend("A") + season("A")),
    auto     = ETS(Trips)
  )
fit_open |> forecast(h = 8) |> autoplot(aus_holidays, level = NULL)

    What should a choosing score reward, and charge for?

    In Session 18 the fitting was already done by ETS(): you wrote the letters and it estimated the weights and initial states. What changes today is who chooses the form. A score that only rewarded fit would always prefer the most flexible model, so the score used to choose a form also charges for every estimated value.

    ETS(Trips) with no specials searches every eligible combination of error, trend and season. Read the model name in the printed mable or in report(); a future software version could choose differently. level = NULL hides the intervals so that the two point forecasts are easy to compare.
    The ETS letters

    Session 19 · The ETS letters

    You have fitted ETS models since Session 15.

    Error: A or M. Trend: N, A or Ad. Season: N, A or M. SES is ETS(A,N,N), Holt ETS(A,A,N), additive Holt-Winters ETS(A,A,A).

    Check: errors in trips, a slope that shrinks further ahead, no season? And ETS(M,N,A) in words?

    ETS(A,Ad,N), damped Holt. ETS(M,N,A): proportional misses, no slope, fixed seasonal effects.

    The order of the letters is a naming convention: error, trend, season. The trend and season letters describe the method's recursions; the error letter describes how unpredictable deviations enter the model.

    Notebook 19_A lists the nine trend and season combinations and reproduces the table of recursions from fpp3 8.4. Multiplicative trends are outside this course.
    From a method to a statistical model

    Session 19 · From a method to a statistical model

    An observation equation turns SES into a statistical model.

    SES, Session 15ETS(A,N,N): a statistical model Predict yt ŷt|t−1 = ℓt−1 Update the level ℓt = ℓt−1 + αet, et = yt − ŷt|t−1 Observation yt = ℓt−1 + εt, εt ~ N(0, σ²), independent
    • The prior prediction, made before yt is observed, becomes the center of a distribution: yt ~ N(ℓt−1, σ²).
    • One surprise moves both: εt moves yt, and αεt moves the level.
    • Future surprises are unknown, so the model gives a distribution of future paths: the forecast intervals.
    The observation equation says how a value could be generated from the previous state and a new shock; the level equation says how that same shock changes the next state. Independent zero-mean normal shocks complete the description.

    A random innovation describes possible observations before they happen. A fitted innovation residual, computed after fitting, estimates the surprise that actually happened at the estimated settings.
    Multiplicative errors

    Session 19 · From a method to a statistical model

    Multiplicative errors: each surprise is a proportion.

    ETS(A,N,N)ETS(M,N,N) Predict yt ŷt|t−1 = ℓt−1 Error et = yt − ŷεt = (yt − ŷ) / ŷ Level ℓt = ℓt−1 + αet(1 + αεt) Observation yt = ℓt−1 + εt(1 + εt)
    With the weights and states held fixed, the error letter leaves the point recursion unchanged. Fitted separately, an additive-error and a multiplicative-error model score the data differently, so their estimates, point forecasts and intervals can all differ.

    Multiplicative errors need strictly positive data. ETS() does not consider them for a series with zeros or negative values.

    augment() returns both residual columns. .resid is always the miss in the data's units. .innov is the miss on the model's own scale: the same number for additive errors, a proportion for multiplicative errors.

    Residual diagnostics for a multiplicative-error model use .innov, because that is the scale on which the model assumes constant spread.
    Likelihood

    Session 19 · Fitting by likelihood

    Likelihood scores each prediction’s distribution at its observation.

    Score: the curve’s height at the observed yt, multiplied over all quarters. ETS() keeps the highest.

    Check: why is a very wide distribution not automatically a good fit?

    Widening lowers the curve where well-predicted observations fall: here from 0.59 to 0.23.

    Likelihood multiplies conditional density heights for the observations. Logarithms turn the product into a sum without changing which parameters maximize it.

    A density height is not the probability of one exact continuous value, and it can exceed one. A wider curve helps a badly predicted observation and hurts the well-predicted ones; adding up all the quarters balances the two, which is how fitting estimates the error variance.

    The notebook's appendix takes one slice through the search, varying alpha alone, and writes the predict, observe, update loop for ETS(M,N,A) in ten lines; it reproduces the log-likelihood that glance() reports.
    AIC and AICc

    Session 19 · Choosing by AICc

    AIC rewards fit but charges for every estimated value.

    AIC = −2 log L + 2k AICc = AIC + 2k(k+1) / (T − k − 1)

    ETS(M,N,A): α, γ, ℓ0, three free seasonal states (the four sum to zero) and σ², so k = 7.

    Check: a trend gives ETS(M,A,A), log L −104.265, k = 9; without it, −106.114 and 7. Which AIC is lower?

    Model−2 log L2kAICAICc
    ETS(M,N,A)212.2314226.23227.78
    ETS(M,A,A)208.5318226.53229.10

    ETS() ranks all 15 eligible forms by AICc. Compare only on the same data and scale.

    The count k includes the error variance as well as smoothing parameters and free initial states. It therefore differs from counts used in some residual-variance corrections.

    AICc adds a finite-sample correction to AIC; BIC, in the notebook's appendix, uses a penalty that grows with the sample size. A small AIC difference is weak evidence either way.

    Fixing part of an ETS specification restricts the candidate set, so an automatic winner is the best eligible fit under the chosen criterion, not a guarantee that every plausible model was considered.

    Changing measurement units shifts every model's log-likelihood by the same constant, so in theory the ranking cannot change. In practice a numerical search can flip a near tie; treat such a gap as a tie and compare forecasts instead.
    Check the selected model

    Session 19 · Check the selected model

    Check the residuals of the selected model.

    fit |> gg_tsresiduals()
augment(fit) |> features(.innov, ljung_box, lag = 8)

    Check: the four checks of Session 9 on residual diagnostics. Where does the model fall short?

    Mean +0.5%; spread grows after 2005; Ljung–Box p = 0.58; roughly symmetric. But 11 of the last 12 are positive: the model lags the rise since 2015.

    gg_tsresiduals() plots innovation residuals, although the histogram's axis is labeled .resid. The distinction matters when you compute statistics yourself from augment().

    A nonsignificant autocorrelation test does not certify every assumption, and a small AICc does not replace looking at correlation, spread and tails. A run of same-signed residuals at the end of the sample matters most, because the forecasts start there.
    The one-step interval by hand

    Session 19 · Forecasting with ETS

    Each forecast is a distribution; its interval comes from the spread.

    2018 Q1: mean 12.695; σ = 0.0464, a proportion.

    StepFormulaPlug in
    Standard deviationŷ × σ12.695 × 0.0464 = 0.589
    95% intervalŷ ± 1.96 × sd12.695 ± 1.96 × 0.589 = [11.54, 13.85]

    2019 Q1 also forecasts 12.70. Same interval?

    No: [11.34, 14.05]. hilo() confirms 2018 Q1.

    For ETS(M,N,A) the forecast distributions are normal and come from a formula; no simulation is needed. The .mean column is the distribution's mean, which is the point forecast.

    For one step ahead with multiplicative errors, the standard deviation is the forecast times sigma. Further ahead the formula includes the effect of future shocks on the level, which is why the intervals widen.

    For some ETS models there is no formula for the forecast variance. Then forecast() simulates many future paths with errors drawn from the assumed distribution and takes percentiles; bootstrapping instead resamples fitted innovations. For nonlinear seasonal models the distribution mean need not equal the recursion with future errors set to zero.

    Before relying on an interval, check whether the innovations support the model's assumptions about correlation and spread.
