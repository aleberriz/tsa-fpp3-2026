    Session 20 · The final exam

    Twenty questions, eighty minutes, one calculator.

    • 10 concept questions, four options each: say what a tool is for and what its output means. Nothing asks you to write R.
    • 10 calculation questions, five options each: short calculations by hand, with the numbers you are given.
    • The whole course is examined. Sessions 15 to 19 on exponential smoothing are the largest block.

    Bring a non-programmable, non-graphing calculator. No phones, watches or other devices, and you cannot leave the room and come back. Answers go on the answer sheet at the back; wrong answers do not subtract, so answer every question. A final below 3.5 out of 10 means the retake, whatever the other grades (see the syllabus).

    The paper is built like the midterm, with one difference: half the questions are calculations. A calculation question gives you every number you need and asks for one result. A concept question asks what a method is for, when it fails, or what a result means for the forecast.

    Session 20 · What to know

    Know the methods by heart; the long equations are printed.

    On the exam: Sessions 1 to 19, everything in the notebooks’ main text and the required homework.

    Not on the exam: notebook appendices, optional demos and readings, optional homework parts, and the fpp3 sections the course leaves out (ARIMA, X-11 and SEATS, Chapter 4 features, Chapter 6 judgmental forecasts, 5.9).

    Know by heart, as in the homework: the benchmark forecasts, moving averages and the classical decomposition steps; MAE, RMSE, MAPE and MASE; σ̂ with T − K, ŷ ± cσ̂ and the naive σ̂√h; the SES update and its weights α(1 − α)j; AIC = −2 log L + 2k.

    Printed when a question needs it: longer equations, such as the Holt, damped and Holt–Winters updates and forecasts, and the normal multipliers. No notes or formula sheet.

    If a formula is not on the by-heart list and is not printed, the question does not need it. The by-heart list is short on purpose: every item is a calculation you have already done in a required homework, with R to check you.

    Session 20 · Practice

    Practice: compare two benchmarks two quarters ahead.

    Beer production, 2008 Q3 to 2010 Q2: 410, 488, 415, 398, 419, 488, 414, 374. Start with the first four quarters, move the origin one quarter at a time, and forecast two quarters ahead.

    StepFormulaPlug in
    Originskeep an origin only if its target is observed2009 Q2, Q3, Q4 → 2009 Q4, 2010 Q1, Q2
    Naivethe last observation at the originforecasts 398, 419, 488; errors 90, −5, −114
    Naive MAE, RMSEmean |e|, √(mean e²)69.67 and 83.91
    Seasonal naivethe same quarter a year before the targetforecasts 488, 415, 398; errors 0, −1, −24; MAE 8.33, RMSE 13.87

    Seasonal naive wins by far: two quarters ahead, the naive forecast carries the wrong quarter’s level into a seasonal series.

    The calculator practice page has seven exercises built on the homework datasets, with numeric checks at the end. Write down the forecast origin, the target and the quantities you need before you calculate; most mistakes happen in that first step.

    Session 20 · Before the exam

    Three things to check first, and where to get help.

    • Fitted values and forecasts, residuals and forecast errors. Only forecasts of unseen data judge a model (Session 8 on benchmarks, Session 13 on train/test accuracy).
    • What Box–Cox cannot fix: swings that grow and shrink while the level stays put (Session 11 on transformations).
    • Predict first, then update, and find the right season for each forecast (Sessions 17 and 18 on Holt and Holt–Winters).

    Help: the calculator practice page and the toolbox reference (Session 19), the notebooks, the midterm calculations with worked solutions (Session 11), and office hours for a concrete question.

    Each of the three has a short section in its notebook: fitted values in 08_A, the Box–Cox limits in 11_A, and the predict-then-update tables in 17_A and 18_A. Read the main text first; the appendices are not needed.
