    14_A › Introduction to the concept

    Where we are  ·  Session 13's test set, three times

    A single test set is one draw, and the verdict can change with where you cut.

    • Session 13 cut the beer series once, at the end of 2007. Cut it a year earlier or later and the test set holds different quarters, with different surprises in them.
    • Accuracy on one split depends on where the split was made. A model can win on one stretch of ten quarters and lose on the next.
    • The fix is the oldest trick in statistics: do it many times and average. That is cross-validation.

    A single test set shows how a model did on one stretch of the future, and an average over many test sets gives a much better idea of how it does in general.

    Why the verdict moves: the test set is a small sample of forecast errors, ten of them in Session 13's example, and ten numbers have a lot of sampling noise. Any single stretch may contain an unusual quarter that favors one model by accident. Cross-validation turns ten errors into hundreds.
    14_A › Cross-validation in time series

    If you have met cross-validation before

    Shuffling time lets the model peek at the future.

    • K-fold draws the test observations at random. That is fine when the rows are independent customers, but wrong when the rows are consecutive months: the model trains on 2019 and is tested on 2017.
    • This is called a leak. Forecasting 2017 with knowledge of 2018 is not really forecasting, so the errors come out too small and say nothing about the future.
    • The rule for time series: a model may never see observations that come after the ones it is tested on. Every split is a train-then-test split in time order.

    The only honest test of a forecast is a future the model did not see. Random folds hand it the future.

    The leak is subtler than it sounds. Even a model with no explicit memory, such as the mean method, learns from the future when a later observation is in the training set: the mean shifts toward where the series will be. Autocorrelation makes it worse, because neighboring observations carry information about each other.

    fpp3 5.10 calls the time series version "evaluation on a rolling forecasting origin". Same thing.
    14_A › Example 1: one-step-ahead forecasts

    fpp3 5.10  ·  the one-step version

    Grow the training set one observation at a time, and test on the next one.

    • Each row is one of Session 13's exercises: fit on the dark dots, forecast the orange one, record the error.
    • The next row adds one observation to the training set and tests on the one after. The window expands; the forecast origin rolls forward.
    • Then average all the errors. Every observation after the first few gets to be a test point once, so the average uses the whole series rather than a single stretch of it.

    Instead of one large test, you run dozens of small ones and average them, following the same rules as in Session 13.

    The first rows are missing on purpose. A training set of two or three observations cannot fit anything sensible, so the first split starts with a minimum size, which is .init in stretch_tsibble().

    With .step = 1 the training set grows by one observation per split. A larger step means fewer splits, which saves time when the models are slow to fit. The benchmark and smoothing models of this course are fast, so step 1 is the default.
    14_A › Example 3: cross-validation for several forecast horizons

    Two variants of the same picture

    Put the test point h steps away, or test the whole stretch from 1 to h at once.

    • Multi-step: if only four-quarter-ahead forecasts matter, test each training set on the observation four steps after it. One error per split, all at h = 4.
    • Several horizons at once: forecast the next four observations from every training set and keep each error with its horizon. This is what the notebook does, and what the homework asks.
    • Two summaries from the same forecasts: the average error per model and horizon, or the average error per model over all horizons.

    The horizon label on each forecast is what makes "which model is best at h = 4" an answerable question.

    Which summary to use: if the decision is always made the same distance ahead, a stock order placed four quarters out for example, the per-horizon table at that horizon is the one that matters. If the forecasts serve many purposes, the average over horizons is the fairer single number. The notebook calls them Option 1 and Option 2.

    Four rules, from the notebook

    Every split is a Session 13 exercise that obeys four rules.

    OrderEvery training observation comes before every test observation.No shuffling. Ever.
    GrowthTraining sets grow forward in time, one step (or a few) at a time..step
    Minimum sizeThe smallest training set is at least as long as the longest horizon, and preferably longer..init ≥ h
    Same lengthAll test sets have the same length, so the errors are comparable across splits.forecast(h = ...)
    • The third rule is the one that gets broken in homework. With .init = 4 and h = 8, the first few splits forecast further ahead than they have data behind them. Make .init comfortably larger than h.
    • The first rule is the one that gets broken by habit, by anyone who has used k-fold before.

    Cross-validation needs only two arguments and a horizon. The rest is bookkeeping, which the next three slides go through.

    A reasonable .init for seasonal data is at least two full seasons, so that a seasonal naive model has a previous season to copy and a second one to be scored on. For the quarterly beer series in the homework, eight observations is the minimum and sixteen is comfortable.
    14_A › The function stretch_tsibble()

    Now we compute  ·  step 1 of 4

    One function builds every training set, and .id is the only new column it adds.

    google_2015_tr <- google_2015 |> stretch_tsibble(.init = 20, .step = 1) |> relocate(Date, .id) google_2015_tr |> pull(.id) |> max() # 233 training sets # Date .id day Close .id = 1 holds days 1 to 20 # 2015-01-02 1 1 524. .id = 2 holds days 1 to 21 # ... .id = 233 holds days 1 to 252
    • .init is the size of the first training set, .step how many observations each next one adds. From a 252-day series, 20 and 1 give 233 training sets.
    • The result is one long tsibble, with every training set stacked under the next and .id saying which set a row belongs to. Day 1 appears 233 times.
    • Nothing else changes: same index, same columns. Every function you know treats .id as one more key, which is exactly what makes the next step one line.

    The function comes from the tsibble package, loaded with fpp3. It only builds the training sets; the forecasting happens in the next step.

    Why 233: the series has 252 trading days; the first set holds 20, and each of the following sets adds one day, up to the full 252. That is 252 minus 20 plus 1 sets.

    The stacked table is large: 233 sets with an average of 136 rows each is about 32,000 rows. For a long series with a slow model, raise .step to keep the number of sets manageable.
    14_A › Step 2: fit the models to each training dataset

    Now we compute  ·  steps 2 and 3 of 4

    One line fits hundreds of models, and the horizon label is the part people forget.

    google_2015_fit_cv <- google_2015_tr |> model(Drift = RW(Close ~ drift()), Mean = MEAN(Close)) # a mable with 233 rows and two model columns: 466 models google_2015_fc_cv <- google_2015_fit_cv |> forecast(h = 8) |> group_by(.id, .model) |> mutate(h = row_number()) |> # 1 to 8 within each set and model ungroup() |> as_fable(response = "Close", distribution = Close) # still a forecast table # 8 x 2 x 233 = 3,728 forecasts, each labeled with its horizon
    • model() fits every model to every .id, because .id is a key. The mable has one row per training set and one column per model.
    • forecast(h = 8) gives eight rows per model per set, in order, but without saying which is the one-step forecast and which the eighth. row_number() inside group_by(.id, .model) adds that label.
    • as_fable() at the end is not optional. After group_by() and mutate() the object has forgotten it is a forecast table; accuracy() needs it to remember.

    If you skip the group_by(), h runs to 3,728, and if you skip as_fable(), the next step refuses the object. Neither mistake shows up until then.

    response names the variable being forecast and distribution names the column that holds the forecast distributions. They are the same name here, Close, because fable names the distribution column after the response.

    14_A › Step 4.1: accuracy by model and horizon

    Now we compute  ·  step 4 of 4, two ways

    With by you get one row per model and horizon, and without it one row per model.

    google_2015_fc_cv |> accuracy(google_2015, by = c(".model", "h")) # 16 rows google_2015_fc_cv |> accuracy(google_2015) # 2 rows # .model h RMSE MAE .model RMSE MAE # Drift 1 11.3 7.2 Drift 24.2 15.3 # Drift 4 23.4 15.2 Mean 87.6 64.7 # Drift 8 31.5 21.1 # Mean 1 85.5 62.6 # Mean 8 89.8 66.8
    • Give accuracy() the original series, google_2015, not the stretched one. It matches each forecast to the observation at the same day, across all 233 sets, and averages.
    • One row of the first table is the average over 233 drift models of their error at that horizon. One row of the second is the average over 233 models and 8 horizons.
    • The columns are Session 13's metrics, MAE, RMSE, MAPE, MASE and the rest, now cross-validated. Read them the same way.

    A warning will say the future dataset is incomplete. It means the last few splits forecast past the end of the data; those forecasts are simply dropped.

    The warning in full: "The future dataset is incomplete, incomplete out-of-sample data will be treated as missing". The last seven training sets end within eight days of the end of the series, so some of their eight-step forecasts have no observation to be compared with. accuracy() skips those; the averages use fewer errors at long horizons than at short ones.
    14_A › Step 4.2: accuracy averaged over horizons

    Google 2015, cross-validated

    Pick the model with the smallest error at the horizon you need.

    • Drift wins everywhere on this series, by a wide margin, and this verdict comes from 233 splits rather than one stretch of days.
    • Drift's error grows with h, as Session 12 said it would: each step ahead adds uncertainty. The mean method's error does not grow, because its forecast is the same flat line at every horizon, and the line is far from where a trending series ends up.
    • The rule: read the per-horizon table at the horizon your decision needs. When no single horizon matters, use the table averaged over horizons.

    Cross-validation does not tell you the truth about the future. It tells you which model has been least wrong about it, many times over.

    Minimizing the cross-validated RMSE picks the model whose forecasts are closest to the mean of the future, following Session 13. For a decision that cares about typical misses rather than large ones, read the MAE column instead.

    Group Assignment 2 ends with exactly this plot for four models on share prices, and the honest answer there is that the naive method is very hard to beat.
