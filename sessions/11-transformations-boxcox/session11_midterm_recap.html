    • The trend T is a 2×4-MA. A 4-MA is centered between two quarters, so we average two neighboring 4-MAs to center the trend on a quarter. Each trend value uses two quarters on each side of t, so the first two and the last two quarters have none.
    • Detrend: D = y − T. At t = 5, D = 38 − 30.5 = 7.5. The quarters with no trend have no D either, which leaves eight values, two for each quarter.
    • One detrended value is not the seasonal component. It holds one year's seasonal effect plus that year's noise. Averaging by quarter removes most of the noise.
    A table like this one, with y and the trend printed, is how the exam presents a decomposition. Here the trend is given; Session 6 shows how to compute it from y.

    A 4-MA does remove the season, because it averages all four quarters. Its only problem is where the result sits in time, and averaging two neighboring 4-MAs fixes that. Strictly, the 2×4-MA is a weighted average of five quarters centered on t, with weights 1/8, 1/4, 1/4, 1/4 and 1/8.

    Average by quarter  ·  the unadjusted seasonal component, as in Session 7, slide 4

    Average every Q1 together, then every Q2, Q3 and Q4.

    • The Q1 value is 7.25. It is not 7.5, the detrended value at t = 5, and it is not 7, the value at t = 9. Each of those is one year's Q1 with that year's noise in it.
    • The averages cancel most of the noise, because the noise pushes one year up and another down. The seasonal component at any t is therefore the value of t's quarter, the same in every year.
    With three years of data and a trend that loses two values at each end, every quarter has two detrended values here. A longer series gives more, and the average is over all of them.

    In R this step is one group_by() on the quarter and one summarise(). The code hides the averaging, which is why the step is easy in R and easy to miss on paper.

    Center  ·  make the seasonal values sum to zero

    Subtract the mean of the four, then check the sum is zero.

    Q1Q2Q3Q4sum
    unadjusted7.25−2.25−1061
    S = unadjusted − 0.257−2.5−10.255.750
    • The four averages add up to 1, so their mean is 1 / 4 = 0.25. Subtract 0.25 from each of them: 7.25 − 0.25 = 7, and so on.
    • Why zero: the season pushes the series up in some quarters and down in others, so over a full year its effects should cancel. A sum of 1 means the season is carrying part of the level, and the level belongs to the trend.
    • Every common mistake fails the check. Subtracting the whole sum gives a total of −3. Adding the mean instead of subtracting it gives 2. Skipping the adjustment leaves 1.

    If the four adjusted values do not add up to zero, the centering went wrong.

    The check takes ten seconds on a calculator and catches every error in this step. Do it every time, in the exam and in R.

    STL against classical decomposition  ·  Session 7

    If the trend is missing at the ends, it is classical. STL has no gaps.

    Classical

    The trend is a moving average, which needs neighbors on both sides, so it is missing at the start and the end of the series. The seasonal pattern is the same every year. An outlier enters every moving average whose window covers it, so it spreads into several trend values.

    STL

    The trend comes from LOESS, so every point has one, the ends included. The seasonal pattern can change from year to year, and the season window controls how fast: the smaller the window, the faster it changes. A robust option keeps outliers in the remainder. In exchange, you choose the trend and season windows by hand.

    • Both methods split y into trend, season and remainder, but they estimate the parts differently. The midterm asked which property belongs to which method.
    • A moving average does not remove an outlier. The outlier enters every window that contains it, so it pulls on several trend values instead of one.
    STL's windows are trend(window = ) and season(window = ). Larger windows give a smoother trend and a more stable seasonal pattern; 07_E shows both.
