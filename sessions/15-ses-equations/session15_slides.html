    Three ways to weight the past

    Session 15 · Simple exponential smoothing

    Naive, mean and SES weight the past differently.

    • Naive uses only the latest observation; the mean gives every observation the same weight.
    • At alpha = 1, SES becomes naive. At alpha = 0, it keeps the initial level; fitting that constant by SSE gives the mean model.
    • For 0 < alpha < 1, recent observations receive more weight and the initial level retains some weight.
    Page 3 of 15_A compares these three weighting rules; 15_B: three methods develops the connection. At alpha = 1, SES gives all observation weight to the latest value and is the naive method.

    At alpha = 0, the level never updates: every fitted value and forecast equals the initial level. Choosing that constant to minimize training SSE gives the sample mean, reproducing the mean model on that sample.

    For any fixed 0 < alpha < 1, older observations receive progressively less weight, even when alpha is small. A smaller alpha makes the weights decline more slowly and the level adapt more slowly. The initial level retains the remaining weight. The mean model gives every observation equal weight.

    SES calculates an estimated level from the observations already available, then uses that level to forecast the next observation. In the equations, y is an observation, ell is a level estimate, and alpha controls how much the latest observation changes that estimate.

    Session 15 · Unpacking the recursive formula

    The error-correction form updates the previous level.

    et = yt − ℓt−1 ℓt = ℓt−1 + α et
    • The previous level ℓt−1 summarizes the history through smoothing; yt−1 is just the last observation. SES uses the previous level as its prediction.
    • The smoothing weight α (alpha), between 0 and 1, determines how much of that residual moves the estimate to its new level, ℓt.
    The previous level summarizes the history through smoothing; the previous observation is just the last data point. SES predicts with the previous level, while the naive method predicts with the last observation.

    Both this error-correction form and the weighted-average form on the next slide are recursive: each calculates the new level from the previous level.

    This diagram reconstructs the training-data updates with the parameters held fixed. Observed minus fitted is a residual; for a genuinely new observation, observed minus its prior forecast is a forecast error. The updating equation works in both settings.

    Recursive means that the new level is calculated from the previous level. The prediction exists before the observation enters the update. If the fitted value was too low, the residual is positive and the level rises; if it was too high, the level falls. Alpha determines the size of that correction.

    Fitted values reconstructed after estimation still use this order inside the recursion. Estimating parameters on a training sample does not turn its fitted values into held-out forecasts.

    Session 15 · Unpacking the recursive formula

    The same recursive update is a weighted average.

    ℓt = ℓt−1 + α(yt − ℓt−1) = αyt + (1 − α)ℓt−1
    • The observation yt receives weight α (alpha), while the previous level ℓt−1 receives 1 − α. Because these weights sum to one, the result is a weighted average.
    • A larger smoothing weight α therefore moves the level further toward the new observation. A smaller weight retains more of the previous estimate.
    The error-correction and weighted-average expressions are two forms of the same recursive update. Expanding the brackets changes its appearance without changing the calculation.

    The recursive form saves work: a shop with years of daily sales can update one level instead of rebuilding a long weighted sum each morning. The previous level already summarizes the earlier observations and the initial estimate.

    Page 18 of 15_A connects this recursion with the exponential moving average used in finance. A chosen moving-average convention sets alpha; fitting SES instead estimates it from a stated objective.

    Session 15 · SES observation weights

    Repeated updates give older observations less weight.

    ℓ1 = αy1 + (1−α)ℓ0 ℓ2 = αy2 + α(1−α)y1 + (1−α)²ℓ0 ℓ3 = αy3 + α(1−α)y2 + α(1−α)²y1 + (1−α)³ℓ0 ℓT = ∑j=0T−1 α(1−α)jyT−j + (1−α)Tℓ0

    Each update multiplies existing weights by 1 − α. After T observations, the initial level retains weight (1−α)T.

    The graph uses the first three observations of the earlier illustrative series: 8, 10 and 7. With alpha = 0.4 and initial level = 9, the updated levels are 8.6, 9.16 and 8.296. Each level is plotted after its observation is incorporated; that level predicts the next observation. The open diamond at t = 0 is a starting estimate, with no observation at that time.

    The first three levels show repeated substitution explicitly. To obtain the third, substitute the expanded second level into the update for the third observation. Every existing weight gains another factor of 1 minus alpha; the newest observation enters with weight alpha.

    The index j counts how many steps back from the latest observation a value lies. Moving back one more period multiplies its weight by 1 minus alpha. The initial level passes through that same multiplication at every update.

    After a finite number of updates the observation weights alone sum to less than one when 0 < alpha < 1. The missing weight belongs to the initial level; it has not disappeared from the model.

    Session 15 · SES observation weights

    Observation weights and the initial-level weight sum to one.

    ST = α + αr + ... + αrT−1, r = 1−α ST − rST = α(1−rT) ST = 1−rT for α > 0

    Observation weights and the initial-level weight sum to one. At alpha = 0 the initial level stays fixed; fitting that constant by squared error gives the sample mean.

    The stacked bar completes the finite average: after two updates at alpha 0.5, the two observations receive 0.5 and 0.25, and the initial level retains 0.25. Leaving out the initial term would leave the weights short of one.

    Page 5 of 15_A derives the geometric sum. Alpha zero needs separate treatment because the division used in that derivation would have a zero denominator; it keeps the chosen initial constant unchanged.

    Session 15 · SES observation weights

    A smaller alpha retains more of the older information.

    Weight on yT−j: wj = α(1−α)j Algerian exports dataset example (α ≈ 0.84): Latest three: 0.84 + 0.84 × 0.16 + 0.84 × 0.16² ≈ 0.996

    Here j = 0 is the latest observation. In the Algerian exports dataset example, the fitted alpha rounds to 0.84. Its latest three observations carry about 99.6% of the weight; the remainder belongs to earlier observations and the initial level.

    The weight on an observation j periods back is alpha times (1 minus alpha) to the power j. Moving one observation farther back multiplies the weight by one minus alpha; it does not subtract a constant amount.

    Page 7 of 15_A shows six decay patterns. Its printed sums cover ten observation weights. The difference from one belongs to initialization, which is visible for a small alpha. Responsiveness alone does not establish overfitting; later forecast errors provide that evidence.

    Session 15 · Fitting and forecasting SES

    SES gives every future period the same point forecast.

    Fitted value: ŷt|t−1 = ℓt−1 Every future horizon h: ŷT+h|T = ℓT

    SES has no persistent trend or seasonality and expects future errors to average zero. Its point forecast stays flat, while accumulating future errors can widen its interval.

    The illustrative history ends at 56, while its calculated final SES level is 52.875, shown as 52.88. The parameters are alpha = 0.5 and initial level = 48. The historical mean, about 49.43, is a reference for comparison. The orange forecast starts at the final smoothed level, separately from the last observation.

    The level lies between the mean and last observation for this chosen series; this is not a general restriction on SES. A flat point forecast does not imply a fixed-width interval. With additive normal errors of standard deviation 2, future uncertainty accumulates while the forecast center stays fixed.

    Session 15 · Fitting and forecasting SES

    Fitting finds the smoothing weight and starting level.

    • For each candidate α (the smoothing weight) and initial level, run the updates on the training data. The sum of squared residuals, SSE, measures how closely that pair fits.
    • Choose the pair with the lowest training SSE, then evaluate forecasts on observations held out of fitting. The evaluation horizon is how far ahead the business decision requires a forecast.
    Fitting estimates the parameters; fitted values are the predictions calculated with them.

    The loop searches over parameter pairs. Each trial replays the predict-residual-update sequence across every training observation, then sums the squared residuals. A new trial starts again from its candidate initial level.

    Squared errors target the conditional mean, as in Session 13 on accuracy measures. They also give a smooth objective for numerical search. For a fixed additive normal-error SES specification on the same observations, likelihood fitting and SSE fitting lead to the same parameter objective.

    Changing alpha in the spreadsheet makes that objective tangible: every update changes, and the squared residuals add to a new total. Session 16 on fitting SES automates this search.

    Session 15 · Algerian exports dataset

    The R specification fits SES to the exports dataset.

    Here algeria_economy contains the annual Algerian exports dataset, with Exports measured as a percentage of GDP.

    fit <- algeria_economy |> model(ETS(Exports ~ error("A") + trend("N") + season("N"))) tidy(fit) augment(fit) forecast(fit, h = 5)
    • The letters specify additive errors (A), no trend (N) and no seasonality (N), giving the SES model.
    • tidy() reports the smoothing weight and starting level. augment() returns fitted values, and forecast() projects five years ahead with prediction intervals.
    The live calculation uses the model fit, fitted values and parameters in 15_B. Writing the newest observation weights on a calculator makes their order clear before inspecting a long printed table.

    Session 15 · Checking the two core ideas

    Check the prediction timing and the recent weights.

    Timing. Previous level 60, observation 68, alpha = 0.25. Which number predicted the observation? What are the residual and the revised level?

    Prediction = 60; residual = 68 − 60 = 8; new level = 60 + 0.25 × 8 = 62

    Weights. At alpha = 0.4, what combined weight do the latest three observations receive? What does the remainder cover?

    0.4 + 0.4 × 0.6 + 0.4 × 0.6² = 0.784

    The remaining 0.216 belongs to older observations and the initial level.

    A useful self-check is to label information by when it becomes available. The current observation can change the new level, but it cannot change the prediction that preceded it.

    For the weights, write the newest term first and multiply repeatedly by one minus alpha. In a finite history, the observation weights and the initial-level weight form the complete average.
