    15_B › Naive vs. mean vs. simple exponential smoothing

    fpp3 8.1  ·  Session 8's benchmarks, seen as weights

    Naive trusts only the last value and the mean trusts all of them equally; SES sits between the two.

    • All three forecast a flat line. The question is only which past values that line is built from, and how much each one counts.
    • The naive method says only yesterday matters; the mean says every day matters the same. Both are extreme, and both are already in your toolbox from Session 8.
    • Simple exponential smoothing is the dial between them: recent observations count most, older ones count less, and nothing is thrown away.

    One number, alpha, sets the dial. Today is about where that number comes from and what it does.

    The handwritten deck 15_A, page 3, draws exactly these three stem plots under the same series. The Algerian exports series in the notebook has no trend and no seasonality, which is the situation all three methods are built for; Session 17 adds trend and Session 18 adds seasonality.

    15_A pages 4 to 6  ·  the only algebra you need

    The weights are a geometric sequence, so they shrink and they add up to one.

    a1 = α r = 1 − α an = α (1 − α)n−1 Sn = a1 + ... + an = 1 − (1 − α)n
    • Each term is the previous one times the ratio, 1 − α. With α between 0 and 1 the ratio is below 1, so the terms shrink toward zero: distant observations get negligible weight.
    • The sum of the first n terms is 1 − (1 − α)n, which tends to 1. So the weights behave like percentages: they add up to the whole.
    • That is what makes SES an average. A weighted average needs weights that sum to one. A geometric sequence starting at α with ratio 1 − α delivers exactly that.

    The whole method follows from these two facts from secondary school, and both are on the final.

    Deriving the sum: write Sn, multiply it by the ratio r, subtract the two, and every term cancels except the first and the last: Sn(1 − r) = a1(1 − rn). With a1 = α and r = 1 − α the fraction simplifies to 1 − (1 − α)n. Page 5 of 15_A does it in four lines.

    Page 7 of 15_A shows the stem plots for six values of alpha from 1 down to 0.1, with the sum of the first ten terms printed on each.
    15_B › Simple exponential smoothing

    15_A page 9  ·  the definition

    The forecast is a weighted average of the whole past, with those weights.

    ŷT+1|T = α yT + α(1−α) yT−1 + α(1−α)² yT−2 + ... + α(1−α)T−1 y1 ŷT+h|T = ŷT+1|T for every h: flat forecasts
    • Read it right to left: the oldest observation, y1, gets the smallest weight; the newest, yT, gets α. Each step back multiplies the weight by 1 − α.
    • The forecasts are flat. Nothing new is observed between T + 1 and T + h, so there is nothing to update, and every horizon gets the same number. That is why SES suits a series with no trend and no season.
    • Two problems with this form, both fixed in the next slides: it is a lot of arithmetic for every single fitted value, and there is no fitted value at t = 1, because there is no past to average.

    This form is easy to read but slow to compute. The recursive form on slide 7 is the one you can actually run.

    Compact notation, from the notebook: the sum over j from 0 to T − 1 of α(1 − α)j yT−j. The index j counts how many steps back from the forecast origin an observation is.

    Why the weights do not sum to exactly 1 on a finite series: the sum of T terms is 1 − (1 − α)T, slightly below 1. With α = 0.84 and T = 58 the gap is invisible; with α = 0.1 and T = 10 it is noticeable, which the 15_A stem plots show.
    15_B › Interpreting the parameters

    What α does  ·  15_A page 7

    A large alpha puts its trust in the latest value, and a small alpha in the long average.

    • α near 1 is almost the naive method: the last observation decides, the rest barely count. The forecast reacts to every new value.
    • α near 0 is almost the mean method: the weights decay so slowly that observations far back still count. The forecast is smooth and slow to react.
    • The data chooses α, by the procedure on slide 10. For Algerian exports it chooses 0.84: a jumpy series, where last year is most of what you know about this year.

    When you report α, explain it in terms of how much of the forecast rests on the last few observations.

    The notebook computes the share for Algeria directly: with α = 0.84, the weights on the three most recent observations are 0.840, 0.134 and 0.022, summing to 0.996. Everything before 2015 shares the remaining 0.4%.

    "Smoothing" is the right word for small α: the fitted values are a smoothed version of the series, much like a moving average from Session 6 but weighted toward the present and using only the past.

    15_A pages 11 and 12  ·  the same thing, runnable

    Each new observation updates the previous forecast, without going back over the whole history.

    ŷT+1|T = α yT + (1 − α) ŷT|T−1
    • Where it comes from. Write the weighted average for T + 1 and for T. Multiply the second by 1 − α and it becomes the tail of the first. Nothing new was assumed; it is the same formula folded up.
    • What it says. The new forecast is a weighted average of two things: the observation that just arrived, and what you expected it to be. α says how much to believe the surprise.
    • Why it matters. It needs one multiplication and one addition per observation, instead of a sum over the whole history. It also needs a starting value, which the next slide names.

    Every exponential smoothing model in the next four sessions is this sentence, with more components: new estimate = α × new information + (1 − α) × old estimate.

    The derivation, page 11 of 15_A: the weighted average for ŷT+1|T starts with α yT and continues with α(1 − α) yT−1 + α(1 − α)² yT−2 + ... Factor (1 − α) out of everything after the first term and what remains inside the bracket is the weighted average for ŷT|T−1.

    The same equation is the exponential moving average used in finance, with α written as 2 divided by one plus the number of days; 15_A pages 16 and 17 make the connection.
    15_B › Component form of the equations

    15_A pages 14 and 15  ·  a name for the forecast

    The level is the running estimate of where the series sits. The forecast is the last level.

    forecast equation ŷT+h|T = ℓT smoothing equation ℓt = α yt + (1 − α) ℓt−1 with ℓ0 as the starting value
    • Define the level at t as the one-step forecast made at t: ℓt = ŷt+1|t. Substitute into the recursive form and it becomes the smoothing equation. This is only a relabeling.
    • Why bother. Session 17 adds a trend equation, Session 18 a seasonal equation. Each component gets its own smoothing equation with its own parameter, and this is the template.
    • ℓ0 is a second parameter. The recursion must start somewhere. Its influence shrinks like (1 − α)t, so it matters at the start of the series and fades.

    With α, ℓ0 and one loop you can reproduce every fitted value and the forecast, and the next slide runs that loop by hand.

    Fitted values from the component form: set h = 1 and shift the time index, so that ŷt|t−1 = ℓt−1. The fitted value at any time is the level one step before. That is the equation the spreadsheet homework uses in its second column.

    Why ℓ0 is estimated rather than set to y1: the notebook's section on fitting treats it as a parameter chosen with α to minimize the SSE. Session 16 uses y1 as the starting guess for the search.
    15_B › Reconstructing the fitted values

    Algerian exports, α = 0.84, ℓ0 = 39.54  ·  the exam calculation of this block

    Two numbers and a loop reproduce every fitted value.

    tytℓt = 0.84 yt + 0.16 ℓt−1fitted ŷt|t−1 = ℓt−1
    039.54  (given)
    139.040.84 × 39.04 + 0.16 × 39.54 = 39.1239.54
    246.240.84 × 46.24 + 0.16 × 39.12 = 45.1039.12
    319.790.84 × 19.79 + 0.16 × 45.10 = 23.8445.10
    ...
    5822.640.84 × 22.64 + 0.16 × 21.43 = 22.4421.43
    59+forecast = ℓ58 = 22.44 for every horizon
    • Each level is a weighted average of the observation and the previous level. Each fitted value is the previous level.
    • The residual is observation minus fitted value: at t = 3, 19.79 − 45.10 = −25.31. Square them, add them up, and you have the SSE the next slide minimizes.
    • The last level is the forecast, the same number at every horizon.

    A few rows of this table, with a calculator, is what the final asks of this block. The homework is all 58 rows in a spreadsheet.

    Rounding: the exam asks for two decimals at every step. Carrying more decimals changes the third row at the second decimal, so follow the instruction rather than your calculator's display.

    The numbers here are fable's own estimates for this series, which is why the table matches augment() exactly in the notebook. With any other α and ℓ0 the loop still runs; the SSE just comes out larger.
    15_B › Finding the best values, that is, fitting the model

    15_A page 13  ·  choosing α and ℓ0

    Fitting means searching for the alpha and initial level with the smallest sum of squared errors.

    • The criterion is least squares, the same as in regression: the parameters that make the one-step errors smallest, in the squared sense.
    • But there is no formula for the answer. Regression has one; SES does not, because α sits inside every term of every fitted value. So the search is numerical: try, measure, adjust, repeat.
    • Session 16 writes this loop in R and hands it to optim(), a general-purpose searcher. Today fable does it for us.

    "The model was fitted" means "a search found the α and ℓ0 that make this table's errors smallest".

    Why the SSE and not the sum of absolute errors: squares make the objective smooth, which the search algorithms need, and minimizing squared errors targets the mean, as Session 13 said. Session 19 replaces the SSE with the likelihood, which for additive errors gives the same α and ℓ0.

    The spreadsheet homework makes the loop tangible: change α in one cell and watch the SSE change.
    15_B › Model specification in fable

    Now we compute  ·  Algerian exports

    ETS(A,N,N) is simple exponential smoothing. tidy() hands you α and ℓ0.

    fit <- algeria_economy |> model(ETS(Exports ~ error("A") + trend("N") + season("N"))) tidy(fit) # alpha 0.840 l[0] 39.54 fit |> augment() # .fitted: the table of slide 9, all 58 rows sum(fitted_vals$weight[1:3]) # 0.996: the last three years carry 99.6% of the weight fit |> forecast(h = 5) # 22.44, five times
    • The three letters: additive error, no trend, no season. Today only the first matters; Sessions 17 to 19 fill in the other two.
    • tidy() returns the parameters, augment() the fitted values. Rebuild the first rows of the table by hand from α and ℓ0 and they match, which is the check the homework asks for.
    • The weights loop turns α into a sentence: with α = 0.84, 99.6% of the forecast rests on the last three observations, and the sum of all the weights is 1 to the third decimal.

    The verbs are the same as in Session 8 (model(), tidy(), augment(), forecast()), and only the model inside has changed.

    The notebook prints the fitted values in reverse order with the weights alongside, so that the most recent observation and its weight of 0.84 are at the top.

    The forecast plot in the notebook overlays the fitted values in orange on the series: smooth where the series is smooth, jumpy where it jumps, because α = 0.84 is close to naive.
