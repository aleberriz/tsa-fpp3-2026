    Where we are  ·  from Session 8, with the rest of the picture

    A forecast is a distribution. The point forecast is its mean, and an interval is a slice of it.

    • Session 8 said it once: the point forecast is the mean of the forecast distribution. Today the rest of that distribution matters.
    • An 80% prediction interval is the stretch of the bell that holds 80% of its area. Eight times in ten, the observation lands inside it.
    • Most models in this course give normal forecast distributions, which is why a bell, two numbers and one table are enough to draw any interval.

    A point forecast on its own says nothing about how far off it might be, and the range is what tells you that.

    The promise of an interval is about the observation, not about the forecast: "the value that will be observed falls in this range with probability 80%". Students often reverse it.

    The notebook draws the real version of this picture twice, with ggdist: the Australian arrivals trend and the Google closing price. Both show the bells standing on the forecast line and widening with the horizon.
    12 › Prediction intervals

    fpp3 5.5  ·  one formula, two ingredients

    An interval is the forecast, plus or minus a multiplier times the spread.

    ŷT+h|T ± c · σ̂h c from the bell curve; σ̂h the spread of the forecast distribution at horizon h
    • The spread, σ̂h. It measures how wide the bell is at horizon h. The next two slides are about where it comes from.
    • The multiplier, c. It sets how much of the bell you want to cover. 1.96 is where the normal curve leaves 2.5% in each tail; 1.28 leaves 10%.
    • The bell itself is an assumption: Session 9's property 4. Residuals that are not normal make c the wrong number.

    Almost all of the difficulty with intervals lies in σ̂h, since the multiplier comes straight from a table.

    Where 1.96 comes from: for a standard normal, 97.5% of the area lies below 1.96, so 2.5% lies above it, and by symmetry 2.5% lies below minus 1.96. The middle 95% sits between them. In R: qnorm(0.975).

    The notebook prints a longer version of the table, for coverages from 50% to 99%, computed with qnorm(1 - alpha/2).
    12 › Intervals for one-step forecasts

    The spread at h = 1  ·  from the residuals

    For one step ahead, the residuals are the forecast errors in rehearsal.

    σ̂ = sqrt( sum(et²) / (T − K) ) T = number of residuals, after dropping NAs K = number of parameters the model estimated: 0 for naive, 1 for mean or drift Google 2015, naive model: T = 251 K = 0 σ̂ = 11.19 ŷ = 758.88 95% interval: 758.88 ± 1.96 × 11.19 = [ 736.95 , 780.81 ]
    • Why the residuals. Each fitted value was a one-step forecast made inside the data, so the residuals are a record of how far one-step forecasts miss. Their standard deviation is the spread you want.
    • Why T minus K. The same correction as a sample variance: each parameter the model estimated used up one residual's worth of freedom.
    • What it needs from Session 9. Constant variance, so that one σ̂ is honest across the whole series; normality, so that 1.96 is the right multiplier.

    The by-hand version needs only a subtraction, a square, a sum and a square root. It takes about a minute, and it is on the final.

    The exam version of this is small: a table of a few residuals, compute the standard deviation, build the interval with 1.96. Practice it on paper once, with the calculator you will bring.

    Why one NA: the naive model's first fitted value does not exist, since there is no previous observation, so Google 2015's 252 trading days give 251 residuals. na.omit() or a logical filter drops it before counting.
    12 › Intervals for multi-step forecasts

    The spread at h > 1  ·  it grows

    Intervals widen as you forecast further ahead, and how fast they widen depends on what the method assumes.

    • Naive widens like √h. Tomorrow is today plus a shock; the day after adds another. Variances add up, so the standard deviation grows with the square root of the number of steps.
    • The mean method barely widens. Its forecast is an average of the whole history, and that average is known well however far ahead you look.
    • For other methods there is no simple formula, but every method has its own way to compute σ̂h, and fable does it. Keep the formulas for reference; the idea to remember is that the interval widens.

    Google at h = 2: σ̂ is 15.82, which is 11.19 times √2. The notebook lets you read it straight off the forecast distribution.

    In the seasonal naive formula, k is the number of complete seasons between the end of the data and the forecast point, the integer part of (h - 1)/m. The interval widens once per season, because each new season copies the previous one and adds one shock.

    Deriving these formulas is not part of this course. What is examinable: that intervals widen with the horizon, that the naive method's width grows with the square root of h, and reading a width off a table of formulas when the table is given.
    12 › Computing prediction intervals with the fable package

    Now we compute

    hilo() gives the interval, and computing it by hand shows that you know where it came from.

    fc <- google_2015 |> model(NAIVE(Close)) |> forecast(h = 5) fc |> hilo() |> unpack_hilo(c(`80%`, `95%`)) # day .mean 80%_lower 80%_upper 95%_lower 95%_upper # 253 758.88 744.54 773.22 736.95 780.81 # 254 758.88 738.60 779.16 727.86 789.90 res <- fit |> augment() |> pull(.innov) |> na.omit() sigma_res <- sqrt(sum(res^2) / (length(res) - nrow(tidy(fit)))) PI <- fc$.mean[1] + c(-1, 1) * qnorm(0.975) * sigma_res all.equal(PI, PI_hilo) # TRUE
    • Three functions. hilo() turns each forecast distribution into intervals; unpack_hilo() spreads them into columns; distributional::parameters() shows the μ and σ of each bell.
    • Then the check. Take the residuals out, drop the NA, apply the formula from two slides ago, and compare the result with the bounds fable gave using all.equal(). This is the same habit as every build-then-verify exercise in the course.
    • The midpoint check is in the notebook too: the mean of the forecast distribution sits exactly between the two bounds. That stops being true after a transformation, later today.

    You will repeat this check alone on an exponential smoothing model in Session 16, so make sure you can follow every step of it today.

    Why nrow(tidy(fit)) is K: tidy() lists one row per estimated parameter, so its row count is the number of parameters. For the naive model that is zero rows.

    parameters() returns one row per horizon with mu and sigma. The sigmas, 11.19, 15.82, 19.38, are the naive formula at h = 1, 2, 3: sigma times the square root of h.
    12 › A more general approach: bootstrapped residuals

    Session 9 again  ·  what the interval assumes

    The interval is only as honest as the residuals behind it.

    Autocorrelation left

    The model is not finished (property 1). Its residuals understate how wrong it will be, and the interval inherits that.

    Spread that drifts

    A single σ̂ for the whole series is wrong (property 3): too wide early, too narrow late. Session 11's transformation is the fix.

    Not normal

    The 1.96 is the wrong multiplier (property 4). Heavy tails make the 95% interval miss more than 5% of the time.

    The escape hatch

    Bootstrapped intervals reuse the model's own past residuals to simulate futures. They need properties 1 and 3, not 4. They take one argument in forecast(), but they are outside this course.

    • So the order of work is fixed: fit, diagnose the residuals, then quote an interval. If you quote it before checking the residuals, you cannot say whether it has the coverage it claims.

    Session 9 told you when a model is finished. Today tells you what you are allowed to claim once it is.

    Bootstrapping, for the curious: draw a residual at random from the model's own past residuals, add it to the one-step forecast, pretend that is the next observation, repeat for h steps, and do the whole thing a few thousand times. The spread of the simulated futures is the interval. In fable: forecast(h = 8, bootstrap = TRUE). fpp3 5.5 covers it; it is not examined.
    12 › Prediction intervals and transformations

    fpp3 5.6  ·  undoing Session 11

    Undoing a log keeps the coverage and breaks the symmetry.

    • Back-transform the ends, not the width. The interval on the log scale is [lower, upper]; on the original scale it is [exp(lower), exp(upper)]. Its coverage is unchanged, because exp keeps every value in the same order.
    • The symmetry does not survive. exp stretches the upper half more than the lower half, so the upper end moves further from the middle than the lower end.
    • The middle moves too. exp of the log-scale mean is the median on the original scale, and the mean sits a little above it, as the next slide explains.

    Session 9 and today agree: a transformation changes the interval far more than it changes the point forecast.

    Why coverage survives: a prediction interval is defined by two quantiles, and any strictly increasing transformation maps quantiles to quantiles. The 10th percentile of the log-scale distribution becomes the 10th percentile of the original-scale distribution. Box–Cox for any lambda is strictly increasing, so the same holds for the whole family.

    The notebook's eggs example draws this with real numbers: a normal bell on the log scale and its back-transformed version on the price scale, side by side for eight horizons.
    12 › Prediction intervals and transformations

    Bias adjustment  ·  which middle you report

    The back-transformed forecast is the median. fable shifts it to the mean, because means add up.

    median on the original scale: exp( ŵT+h|T ) mean on the original scale: exp( ŵT+h|T ) · [ 1 + σh² / 2 ] σh² on the log scale egg prices, drift on log(eggs): h = 1 mean 61.8 median 61.3 h = 50 mean 47.0 median 27.9
    • Why they differ. The bracket is bigger than 1 and grows with the forecast variance. At h = 1 the variance is small and the two almost agree; at h = 50 it is large and they are far apart.
    • Why anyone wants the mean. If you forecast ten regions and add them up, the sum of the medians is not the median of the sum. The sum of the means, however, is the mean of the sum.
    • What fable does. It reports the mean by default, which is called bias-adjusting. median() on the distribution column gives you the median back.

    Both the mean and the median are legitimate. Report the one your decision needs, and say which one it is.

    The bracket formula is the log case. For a general Box–Cox lambda the notebook gives the longer version; the shape is the same, exp-like back-transformation times one plus something proportional to the forecast variance.

    The median is often the better choice for a single series read by people: it is the value the series is equally likely to fall above or below. The mean is the better choice for anything that will be summed, averaged or compared with a budget.
    12 › Understanding the forecasts returned when using transformations

    Now we compute

    Write the log inside the model formula, and fable undoes everything for you.

    # model 1: a column you logged yourself. Forecasts come back in log units. fit1 <- prices |> model(RW(log_eggs ~ drift())) # model 2: the log declared in the formula. Forecasts come back in cents, bias-adjusted. fit2 <- prices |> model(RW(log(eggs) ~ drift())) fc2 |> autoplot(prices, level = 80, point_forecast = lst(mean, median))
    • The fit is the same, but the forecast table is not. Model 1 does not know a transformation happened. Model 2 does, so it back-transforms the distribution, the intervals and the point forecast.
    • The notebook runs three checks, and all three return TRUE. exp of model 1's bounds equals model 2's bounds; exp of model 1's mean equals model 2's median; the mean of model 2's distribution equals its .mean.
    • The same rule holds inside decomposition_model(): STL(log(Turnover) ~ ...) and the forecasts come back in turnover units, which is what the homework uses.

    One rule covers every transformation in this course: put it in the formula, not in a new column.

    point_forecast = lst(mean, median) asks autoplot() to draw both middles; they are drawn in different line types. On the log scale they coincide; on the original scale they separate as h grows.

    Box–Cox works the same way: RW(box_cox(y, 0.3) ~ drift()). The lambda has to be a number in the formula, so compute it with guerrero first and pass it in.
