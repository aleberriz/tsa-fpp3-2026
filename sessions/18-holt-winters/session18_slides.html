    18_A › Motivation

    Where we are  ·  Session 7's components, updated one observation at a time

    Holt–Winters is Holt plus a seasonal index for each period of the year.

    • Session 7 split a series into trend, season and remainder, once, looking at the whole series at the same time.
    • Holt–Winters keeps the same three pieces but updates them one observation at a time, so it can forecast: a level, a slope, and an index for every quarter or month of the year.
    • Three dials: α for the level, β* for the slope, and a new one, γ, for the seasonal indices.

    Holt–Winters turns Session 7's decomposition into a forecasting machine.

    m is the length of the season: 4 for quarterly data with a yearly pattern, 12 for monthly, 7 for daily data with a weekly pattern. Every seasonal term in today's equations looks back m steps.
    18_A › Additive vs multiplicative

    Session 5's question, with a consequence

    If the swings stay the same size, add the season; if they grow with the level, multiply.

    • Additive: the season adds a fixed amount, "Q1 is 1.5 million trips above the level". The indices add up to about zero over a year.
    • Multiplicative: the season scales the level, "Q1 is 16% above the level". The indices add up to about m over a year, 4 for quarterly data.
    • The eye test is Session 5's: if the envelope opens like a funnel, multiply. Alternatively, log the series first, as in Session 11, and add.

    It is the same choice as in Session 5, except that now the forecasts depend on it.

    Why the sums: an additive seasonal component is a deviation from the level, so over a full year the deviations cancel; a multiplicative one is a ratio to the level, so over a year the ratios average to one. Session 7's centering step enforces the same thing in a classical decomposition.
    18_A › Component form of the equations

    fpp3 8.3  ·  the additive method

    Every seasonal term looks back one full year: st−m.

    forecast ŷt+h|t = ℓt + h bt + st+h−m(k+1) level ℓt = α (yt − st−m) + (1 − α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1) deseasonalized observation vs non-seasonal forecast trend bt = β*(ℓt − ℓt−1) + (1 − β*) bt−1 Holt's, unchanged season st = γ (yt − ℓt−1 − bt−1) + (1 − γ) st−m this year's surprise vs last year's index
    • The level now sees the observation with its season taken out, so that a strong Q1 does not look like a jump in the level.
    • The seasonal index for this quarter is a weighted average of what this quarter just showed, above the old level and slope, and what the same quarter showed one year ago.
    • Fitted value: ℓt−1 + bt−1 + st−m. This is Holt's fitted value plus last year's index for this quarter.

    All three smoothing equations follow the same sentence, and only the bookkeeping of seasons is new.

    The inherited notebook wrote the first fitted value with s1+1−m; it is s1−m, the index of the same quarter in the year before the data. Corrected in this build.

    Starting values: ℓ0, b0, and m seasonal indices for the year before the data, s1−m to s0. For quarterly data that is six starting values plus three smoothing parameters.
    18_A › The k subindex in the seasonal component

    The easiest thing to get wrong  ·  take it slowly

    k only makes sure every forecast uses its season's index from the last observed year.

    k = int( (h − 1) / m ) index used: sT+h−m(k+1) m = 4, h = 1: k = 0 T + 1 − 4 = T − 3 last year's Q1 m = 4, h = 5: k = 1 T + 5 − 8 = T − 3 last year's Q1 again
    • k counts the complete years between the last observed year and the forecast. int( ) drops everything after the decimal point.
    • Its only job is to step back the right number of years, so the index always comes from data the model has seen. Without it, h = 5 would ask for an index from the future.
    • Session 8's seasonal naive did exactly this lookup. Holt–Winters adds the level and slope on top.

    In words, a forecast for a Q1 uses the latest Q1 index there is, and the formula says the same thing in symbols.

    The notebook draws the monthly version, m = 12: the forecast for any January, however far ahead, uses the January index of the last observed year.

    18_A › Holt-Winters multiplicative method

    The other variant

    Multiplicative: divide where you subtracted, multiply where you added.

    forecast ŷt+h|t = (ℓt + h bt) · st+h−m(k+1) level ℓt = α yt / st−m + (1 − α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1) trend bt = β*(ℓt − ℓt−1) + (1 − β*) bt−1 season st = γ yt / (ℓt−1 + bt−1) + (1 − γ) st−m
    • The level and slope still add; only the season multiplies. Deseasonalizing is a division instead of a subtraction.
    • Use multiplicative errors with it. Additive errors with a multiplicative season are numerically unstable; in ETS(), write error("M") with season("M"). Session 19 lists the combinations that are excluded and why.
    • The fitted-value equation is yours to derive: set h = 1 and shift back one step, as for the additive case. The notebook has the multiplicative table to check against.

    The machine and the order of operations stay the same; the only question is whether the season is an amount or a percentage.

    fpp3 8.3 gives both variants. The exercise plots the prediction intervals of the additive and the multiplicative model one after the other: compare how the width of each behaves as the series grows, and ask which one suits a series whose swings grow with its level.
    18_A › Code

    Now we compute  ·  Australian holiday trips, millions

    AAA and MAM fit almost equally well, but tidy() hides one catch.

    fit <- aus_holidays |> model( additive = ETS(Trips ~ error("A") + trend("A") + season("A")), multiplicative = ETS(Trips ~ error("M") + trend("A") + season("M"))) accuracy(fit) # RMSE 0.417 vs 0.412 tidy(fit) # additive: alpha 0.262 beta 0.0431 gamma 0.0001 l[0] 9.79 b[0] 0.02 s[0]...s[-3]
    • The two fits are close: training RMSE 0.417 against 0.412. Holiday trips are only mildly multiplicative.
    • components() shows level, slope and season for each; the panel titles in this build say ETS(A,A,A) and ETS(M,A,M).
    • The catch: tidy() prints beta = α × β*, here 0.262 × 0.1646 = 0.0431. The workbook's 0.1646 is β*. Both are right; they are different numbers.

    That makes nine numbers estimated for one quarterly series, three dials and six starting values, and the next slide comes back to that count.

    Why fable reports αβ*: it fits the model in Session 19's state space form, where the slope is updated by the one-step error times a parameter called β. Rewriting the component form shows that β = αβ*. The same holds for Holt's method in Session 17, where α was almost 1 and the two looked the same.

    Small γ and small β both say the same thing about this series: the seasonal pattern and the trend have been stable for twenty years.
    18_A › Code

    A warning that comes with the season

    Every season adds m starting values, and each extra parameter makes a good fit harder to trust.

    Quarterly, m = 43 dials + level + slope + 4 seasons9 numbers
    Monthly, m = 123 dials + level + slope + 12 seasons17 numbers
    Weekly, yearly season3 dials + level + slope + 52 seasons57 numbers
    • Every one of them is fitted to the same data, and every extra one makes it easier to fit the training set and harder to forecast well: Session 13's overfitting.
    • "With four parameters I can fit an elephant, and with five I can make him wiggle his trunk." John von Neumann, as quoted by Enrico Fermi and told by Freeman Dyson in Nature in 2004.
    • Session 19's criteria, AIC and its cousins, exist to charge a model for each parameter it uses. Today's models are why they are needed.

    A better fit is not a better model until it has paid for its parameters.

    Strictly, the m seasonal starting values are constrained to sum to zero (additive) or m (multiplicative), so one of them is not free. The point stands: the count grows with m.
    18_A › Holt-Winters damped method

    Session 17's damping, with a season

    Damped trend with a multiplicative season is often the best single method for seasonal data.

    ETS(Count ~ error("M") + trend("Ad") + season("M")) # Southern Cross pedestrians, daily, m = 7
    • The trend is damped exactly as in Session 17: φ multiplies every slope carried forward, and the forecast stops climbing.
    • The season is a weekly one: daily data, m = 7. That means seven starting indices, one per weekday.
    • The notebook fits one month and forecasts two weeks; the forecasts follow the held-out weeks closely, weekday pattern and rising trend included.

    With a level, a damped slope and a season, this is the most complete model of the block, and it is still just three smoothing sentences and a lookup.

    fpp3 8.3 calls damped multiplicative Holt–Winters "often the single most accurate forecasting method for seasonal data". Session 19 lets ETS() decide whether it is for a given series.
