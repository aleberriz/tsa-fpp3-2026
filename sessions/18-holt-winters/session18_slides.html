    Session 18 · Exponential smoothing with seasonality

    Seasonal effects can be fixed amounts or proportional changes.

    • In the illustrative series on the left, swings stay similar in size as the level rises. An additive seasonal index measures that effect in the data's units, so seasonal adjustment subtracts it.
    • On the right, swings grow with the level. A multiplicative seasonal index expresses the effect as a factor, so seasonal adjustment divides by it.
    The seasonal period m is the number of observations in one cycle: four quarters in a year, twelve months in a year, or seven days in a week. It is determined by the pattern and observation frequency, not by the forecast horizon.

    Additive seasonal starting effects are centered around zero; multiplicative factors are centered around one. This is the same identification idea used in Session 7 on decomposition: the seasonal component should not also carry the overall level.

    Session 18 · Updating seasonal estimates

    Predict the observation, then update its underlying level.

    Observation prediction: ŷt|t−1 = ℓt−1 + bt−1 + st−m Adjusted observation = yt − st−m Prior nonseasonal prediction = ℓt−1 + bt−1 ℓt = α × adjusted observation + (1−α) × prior prediction Slope update: bt = β*(ℓt−ℓt−1) + (1−β*)bt−1

    The target yt is the observed series. Level ℓ summarizes its underlying size, slope b its change per period, and season s the recurring effect. With m = 4 quarters per cycle, st−m is the matching old seasonal state. The weights α and β* control revision of level and slope.

    Before the first observation, the model needs an initial level, slope and a seasonal index for each period in a cycle. The first observation uses the seasonal index from its own season in that initial cycle.

    The adjusted observation can then update the underlying level without treating a recurring seasonal peak as a permanent increase. The slope update follows the same logic as Holt once that adjustment has been made.

    The model predicts the observed series by combining its underlying level, slope and matching seasonal state. Updating the level alone is only part of that job.

    Session 18 · Updating seasonal estimates

    The seasonal update blends a new effect with the old index.

    New seasonal estimate = yt − (ℓt−1 + bt−1) st = γ × new seasonal estimate + (1−γ)st−m
    • Subtract the previous level ℓt−1 and slope bt−1 from observation yt to estimate this period's seasonal effect.
    • The weight γ (gamma) blends that estimate with st−m, the index from one cycle of m periods earlier, to obtain st. Small γ therefore makes the seasonal pattern change slowly.
    Small gamma means the seasonal profile changes slowly; it does not mean the seasonal effect itself is small. A large recurring peak can retain almost the same index from one cycle to the next.

    The equation here uses the previous level and slope to measure the new seasonal effect. Some texts rewrite the update using a different parameter convention, so equations and parameter definitions must be read together.

    Session 18 · Updating seasonal estimates

    One forecast error can revise all three components.

    Illustrative row: old level 30, slope 1, matching season −2; actual 33. Use α = 0.5, β* = 0.2, γ = 0.25. Calculate the prior prediction, then level, slope and season.

    Prediction = 30 + 1 − 2 = 29 Level = 0.5(33+2) + 0.5(31) = 33 Slope = 0.2(33−30) + 0.8(1) = 1.4 Season = 0.25(33−30−1) + 0.75(−2) = −1

    The seasonal update uses the old level and slope, even though the new level has already been calculated.

    In the fitted holiday-trip workbook gamma is very small, so a seasonal update can disappear at the displayed precision. The moderate gamma in this calculator example makes the blending visible.

    The additive sheet starts with four seasonal indices before the first observed quarter. Its slope weight is beta-star; when checking against tidy(), remember that the reported beta is alpha times beta-star.
    Forecast indexing

    Session 18 · Matching the forecast season

    Forecasts reuse the target season's index.

    ŷT+h|T = ℓT + hbT + sT+h-m(k+1) k = floor((h−1)/m)

    Here h is the horizon, m the cycle length, and k the completed forecast cycles before the target. Retrieve the latest observed instance of the target season. Check: the sample ends in Q2; which quarter and stored index do you need at h = 5, m = 4?

    The last m observed seasonal indices form a rolling calendar, even if the series ends halfway through a year. A future January uses the latest observed January index, which need not belong to the last complete calendar year.

    Horizons one full cycle apart reuse the same seasonal index. Their level-and-trend contributions can still differ because more time has elapsed. That separates the seasonal lookup from the forecast's underlying path.

    The calendar strip ends in Q2. Its last four observed indices span two calendar years. At h = 5, the target is Q3 and k = floor((5−1)/4) = 1, so T + 5 − 4(1 + 1) = T − 3. Both the first and fifth future quarters reuse that latest observed Q3 index.

    Multiplicative equations

    Session 18 · Comparing seasonal models

    A multiplicative seasonal factor scales the underlying forecast.

    Remove season: yt / st−m Restore season: (ℓT + hbT) × matching seasonal factor

    To adjust observation yt, divide by st−m, its seasonal factor from one cycle of m periods earlier. To forecast h periods beyond the last observation T, multiply the projected level ℓT + hbT by the matching factor. A factor of 1.2 puts the forecast 20% above that underlying path.

    A seasonal factor expresses a ratio, so multiplying it by the underlying path makes the seasonal swing grow with the level. Dividing an observation by its old factor removes that expected seasonal scaling before the update.

    The notebook and fpp3 8.3 give the full multiplicative equations as supporting reading. Comparing fitted plots helps interpret them, but forecast accuracy and interval coverage require later observations.
    Australian holiday-trips dataset in R

    Session 18 · Comparing seasonal models

    The two seasonal specifications express different assumptions.

    The Australian domestic holiday-trips dataset records quarterly overnight trips in millions. This comparison changes both the error and seasonality choices: two complete specifications, with an additive trend in both.

    additive = ETS(Trips ~ error("A") + trend("A") + season("A")) multiplicative = ETS(Trips ~ error("M") + trend("A") + season("M"))
    • The A and M error letters select additive or multiplicative errors. Both models retain an additive trend, while the season letter changes how the seasonal pattern scales.
    • When checking the workbook, use beta-star for its slope weight: tidy() reports beta = alpha × beta-star, where alpha is the level weight.
    In the state space form, the slope is updated using the one-step error and a coefficient called beta. Rewriting the component equation gives beta = alpha times beta-star. When alpha is well below one, the two values can look quite different.

    Small gamma means a stable seasonal profile, while a small slope weight means slow revision of the trend estimate. Neither value measures the size of the seasonal effect or the slope itself.

    The AAA-versus-MAM fit changes both error and seasonal choices. It compares complete specifications; the result cannot isolate the effect of seasonality alone. Supplied workbook states use rounded precision; fitted states and optimizer guesses have different roles.

    Session 18 · Comparing seasonal models

    Forecast errors can challenge the model suggested by a plot.

    A series' seasonal pattern suggests which models to try. Their performance on later observations tells you how useful those choices are for the required forecast horizon.

    • Training RMSE, the root mean squared error, measures fit to observations the model has already used. Compare held-out errors to judge forecasting.
    • A narrow interval may reflect stronger assumptions. Check whether intervals contain later observations as often as their stated coverage suggests.
    A seasonal model needs starting indices for its cycle as well as level and trend information. More flexibility can improve fit without guaranteeing better forecasts, which motivates the selection criteria in Session 19 on ETS estimation and selection.

    Damped seasonal methods are also candidates. The notebook's daily example and parameter-count discussion are further reading; held-out accuracy at the relevant horizon remains the way to judge a forecasting decision.
