    Session 17 · Holt's level and slope

    Holt predicts from the level and slope already available.

    ŷt|t−1 = ℓt−1 + bt−1

    The forecast ŷt|t−1 predicts time t using information through t−1. It adds the previous level ℓt−1 to the previous slope bt−1, the estimated change per period. Only after observing time t can Holt revise those estimates.

    The drift benchmark from Session 8 on forecasting methods also projects a slope. Holt differs by updating its level and slope as each observation arrives, allowing the estimated trend to evolve.

    A slope is measured in data units per period. For an annual population series in millions, it is millions per year; confusing that with a percentage growth rate changes the interpretation of every forecast.

    Session 17 · Holt's level and slope

    Holt blends new information with its previous estimates.

    Level update: ℓt = αyt + (1−α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1) Slope update: bt = β*(ℓt−ℓt−1) + (1−β*)bt−1
    • The level ℓt combines observation yt with the earlier forecast, previous level plus slope. The smoothing weight α determines how much the observation contributes.
    • The slope bt combines the change in level with the old slope bt−1. Its smoothing weight β* (beta-star) determines how much that new change contributes.
    The star distinguishes the component-form slope weight from the parameter called beta in fable. Rewriting the update in error-correction form gives beta = alpha times beta-star.

    The fitted value uses the previous level plus the previous slope. The new slope uses the newly updated level, so moving the slope calculation before the level calculation changes the method.

    Session 17 · Holt's level and slope

    An observation above the forecast raises both estimates here.

    Old level = 80, old slope = 2, observation = 86 α = 0.5, β* = 0.3 Prediction = 82 New level = 0.5 × 86 + 0.5 × 82 = 84 New slope = 0.3 × (84−80) + 0.7 × 2 = 2.6

    In this illustrative example, α weights the observation when revising the level, while β* weights the change in level when revising the slope. The observation exceeds the earlier prediction, so both estimates rise.

    The population workbook uses a fitted alpha very close to one. Its updated level therefore almost equals each observation, making the contribution of the earlier prediction difficult to see. The moderate weight on this slide makes both contributions visible.

    Workbook 17_B starts with a time-zero level and slope, then has one row per observation and later forecast rows. The prediction column must use states from the preceding row.

    Session 17 · Holt's level and slope

    A responsive slope can follow a trend change or a fluctuation.

    The smoothing weight β* (beta-star) controls how quickly Holt revises its slope. A large value can help when the underlying trend changes, but can also follow a temporary fluctuation.

    • Check whether the remaining forecast errors contain predictable patterns.
    • Compare forecasts against later observations at the horizon of interest, because the parameter value alone cannot establish overfitting.
    The notebook has an interactive slope-weight example: a slider moves β* from 0 to 1 with α fixed at 0.4 on the Australian population series. Worth a minute on your own.

    A changing slope is not automatically an overfitted slope. The distinction depends on whether its changes help predict observations outside the fitting sample at the horizon of interest.

    Session 17 · Australian population dataset

    Holt projects the final slope at a constant rate.

    ŷT+h|T = ℓT + hbT

    For the annual Australian population dataset, measured in millions, ℓT is the final estimated level and bT the final slope. The forecast ŷT+h|T adds h periods of that slope. Use components() to inspect the estimates and check how they produce the straight forecast path.

    The fitting loop is the SES loop with an initial slope and a slope weight added. Once fitted, components() exposes the internal level and slope so that the forecast can be reconstructed.

    These states differ from the centered smooths used in Session 7 on classical decomposition. They evolve one observation at a time according to the forecasting model. The name ETS(A,A,N) records additive errors, an additive trend and no seasonality.

    Session 17 · Damped trend forecasts

    Damping flattens both rising and falling forecast paths.

    The damping factor φ (phi) reduces the slope's contribution at each successive horizon. With the same starting level and slope, the damped forecast therefore lies below a rising Holt path and above a falling one.

    A damping factor close to one lets the estimated trend persist for longer. A smaller factor suppresses its contribution sooner. The usual estimation range, 0.8 to 0.98, avoids very rapid flattening while keeping genuine damping.

    The comparison here holds the starting states fixed. Separate fitted models may estimate different levels and slopes as well as different damping, so their actual forecasts need to be inspected rather than ordered by a rule about phi alone.

    Session 17 · Damped trend forecasts

    The damped forecast approaches a finite limit.

    ŷT+h|T = ℓT + (φ + φ² + ... + φh)bT φ + ... + φh = φ(1−φh)/(1−φ) As h grows: ŷ approaches ℓT + φbT/(1−φ)

    At the final observation T, ℓT is the level and bT the slope. The forecast ŷT+h|T is h periods ahead. With damping factor 0 < φ < 1, each added power is smaller, so the geometric sum, and therefore the forecast, approaches a limit.

    The geometric sum and the forecast have different limits: the sum approaches phi divided by one minus phi; multiplying by the final slope and adding the final level gives the forecast limit.

    With a positive slope the forecast approaches that limit from below. With a negative slope it approaches from above. At phi equal to one the finite sum is simply the horizon, giving Holt; the fraction written for phi below one cannot be used at that endpoint.

    Session 17 · Fitting a damped trend

    The default damped model estimates how quickly the trend fades.

    In the annual Australian population dataset, Pop measures population in millions. This specification combines additive errors, a damped additive trend and no seasonality.

    ETS(Pop ~ error("A") + trend("Ad") + season("N"))
    • The damping factor φ is estimated between 0.8 and 0.98 by default. A value closer to one lets the trend persist longer.
    • Compare the resulting forecasts with Holt's. If the estimate reaches a bound, examine how that restriction affects the forecast before changing it.
    Damping also enters the state updates: a slope carried one period forward is multiplied by phi. The notebook gives those equations and optional examples with a fixed damping factor or a changed estimation range.

    A boundary estimate is a reason to inspect the restriction and the forecasts. Rolling-origin comparisons can then ask whether a different specification improves accuracy at the required horizon.
