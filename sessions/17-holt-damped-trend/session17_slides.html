    17_A › Motivation

    Where we are  ·  what SES cannot do

    A flat forecast of a rising series is wrong, and more wrong every step ahead.

    • SES forecasts the last level, forever. That is fine for Algerian exports, which wander with no direction, but wrong for anything that grows.
    • Session 8's drift method had a slope, but a fixed one: the average change over the whole history. Holt's slope is smoothed like the level, so it can change.
    • The recipe is Session 15's, applied twice: one smoothing equation for the level, one for the slope, each with its own dial.

    Holt's method adds one line to SES, and everything you know about the level carries over.

    Holt published the method in 1957. It is still in daily use for inventory and demand forecasting, mostly in its damped form, which is the second half of today.
    17_A › Component form of the equations

    fpp3 8.2  ·  the component form

    Two smoothing equations, two dials: α for the level, β* for the slope.

    forecast ŷt+h|t = ℓt + h bt level ℓt = α yt + (1 − α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1) new observation vs what you expected trend bt = β*(ℓt − ℓt−1) + (1 − β*) bt−1 new slope vs old slope
    • The level equation is SES, except that "what you expected" is now the old level plus the old slope: if the series was climbing, you expected it to keep climbing.
    • The trend equation smooths the slope. ℓt − ℓt−1 is how much the level just rose; β* says how much to believe that rise over the old slope.
    • Four numbers to estimate: α, β*, and two starting values, ℓ0 and b0. The search is the same as in Session 16, with a longer vector.

    Both smoothing equations say the same sentence: new estimate = weight × new information + (1 − weight) × old estimate.

    Both parameters lie between 0 and 1. The star on β* is there because Session 19 rewrites the same model with a parameter called β that is a different number (β = αβ*); the star marks the one in these equations.

    Fitted values: set h = 1 and shift back one step, so ŷt|t−1 = ℓt−1 + bt−1. The previous level plus the previous slope.

    17_A › Effect of the value of beta*

    The second dial

    A large β* lets the slope chase noise, which is overfitting under a new name.

    • β* near 0: the slope barely moves from b0. The trend is treated as permanent; the forecast is close to a fixed line.
    • β* near 1: the slope is whatever the last rise was. Every bump in the data becomes a new trend, and the forecast swings with it.
    • The data chooses, by the same search as before. A small fitted β* says the trend has been steady; a large one says it has been changing.

    Session 13's warning, again: a fit that follows every point has learned the noise.

    The notebook has an interactive version of this picture: a slider moves β* from 0 to 1 with α fixed at 0.4 on the Australian population series. Worth a minute on your own.

    Session 16's loop  ·  a longer vector

    Fitting Holt is Session 16's search over four numbers instead of two.

    • Guess α, β*, ℓ0, b0; run the three equations down the series; add the squared one-step errors; adjust; repeat until the SSE stops falling.
    • We do not code it again. my_ses_sse() with a third line in the loop and a par of four would do it; ETS() does it for us.
    • The workbook 17_B is one pass of the loop, with the parameters fable found. Filling it in is the homework: it is the step the search repeats hundreds of times.

    More components mean more starting values, and more numbers for the search to find. Session 18 adds a whole season of them.

    17_A › R example: Australian population

    Now we compute

    trend("A") is Holt. components() hands you the level and slope columns.

    fit <- aus_economy |> model(AAN = ETS(Pop ~ error("A") + trend("A") + season("N"))) tidy(fit) # alpha 1.00 beta 0.327 l[0] 10.05 b[0] 0.222 components(fit) # Year, level, slope: 1959 is time 0 holts_components |> mutate(.fitted = lag(level) + lag(slope)) # the fitted-value equation all.equal(fitted_augment, fitted_components) # TRUE fit |> forecast(h = 10) # 24.97, 25.34, 25.71, ...: a straight line
    • The parameters are the workbook's. One catch: tidy() prints beta = α × β*, not β* itself. With α almost 1, as here, the two coincide; Session 18's seasonal model is where they differ.
    • components() returns a dable: the level and slope at every year, plus time 0. lag(level) + lag(slope) rebuilds every fitted value, and all.equal() confirms it.
    • The forecasts are the last level plus h slopes, as the forecast equation says: a straight line that never bends.

    As before, the workbook is the calculation by hand, and this slide checks it against the library.

    The model name AAN anticipates Session 19's notation: additive error, additive trend, no season. ETS(A,A,N) is Holt's linear method.

    components() on an ETS model is not a decomposition in Session 7's sense: the level and slope are the model's internal states, updated one observation at a time, not a centered smooth of the series.
    17_A › Damped trend methods

    fpp3 8.2  ·  the damped trend

    Few trends last forever, so damping bends the line toward flat.

    • Holt's forecast climbs at the same rate forever. Over ten or twenty steps that usually overshoots: growth slows, markets saturate, prices stop rising.
    • One more parameter, φ, between 0 and 1, shrinks the slope a little more at every step ahead. φ = 1 is Holt; φ between 0.8 and 0.98 is the usual range.
    • Damped trend is one of the most accurate methods there is in forecasting competitions, for exactly this reason. It is the model Group Assignment 2 uses.

    Damping keeps the recent trend and gradually flattens it the further ahead you forecast.

    Below 0.8 the damping is so strong that the forecast is almost flat after a few steps; above 0.98 it is almost Holt. That is why fable and fpp3 restrict φ to that range when estimating it.
    17_A › Damped trend methods

    Session 15's algebra, with ratio φ

    The damping factors are a geometric sequence, so their sum stops growing.

    Holt ŷT+h|T = ℓT + h bT damped ŷT+h|T = ℓT + (φ + φ² + ... + φh) bT φ + φ² + ... + φh → φ / (1 − φ) as h grows so the forecast → ℓT + φ bT / (1 − φ) φ = 0.9: h = 1: 0.9 h = 2: 1.71 h = 5: 3.69 h = 10: 5.86 limit: 9
    • Holt multiplies the slope by h, which grows without end. Damping multiplies it by a sum of powers of φ, which grows ever more slowly.
    • That sum is a geometric series with first term and ratio φ. Below 1, it converges to φ / (1 − φ): 9 for φ = 0.9, 49 for φ = 0.98.
    • So the forecast levels off at ℓT + φ bT / (1 − φ): the last level plus a finite amount of extra growth.

    By hand, the two-step damped forecast is ℓT + (φ + φ²) bT, and you should expect a line like this on the final.

    The inherited notebook said the geometric sum itself converges to ℓT + φ bT / (1 − φ); it is the forecast that does. Corrected in this build.

    Australian population, φ estimated at 0.98: the forecast tends to about 42 million, against 24.6 in 2017. A long way, because 0.98 damps gently.
    17_A › Damped trend methods

    The full component form  ·  fpp3 8.2

    Every time the slope is carried one step forward, it is multiplied by φ.

    forecast ŷt+h|t = ℓt + (φ + φ² + ... + φh) bt level ℓt = α yt + (1 − α)(ℓt−1 + φ bt−1) trend bt = β*(ℓt − ℓt−1) + (1 − β*) φ bt−1
    • Compare with slide 3: the only change is a φ in front of every old slope. Set φ = 1 and Holt comes back.
    • The fitted value is ℓt−1 + φ bt−1: the one-step forecast of the damped model.
    • Five numbers to estimate: α, β*, φ, ℓ0, b0. In ETS() that is trend("Ad"): additive, damped.

    In the formula the only change is "Ad" instead of "A", and there is one more parameter to read.

    The inherited notebook showed only the damped forecast equation; the level and trend equations are added in this build from fpp3 8.2.
    17_A › Damped example 1

    Now we compute  ·  Australian population, 15 years ahead

    You can fix φ, bound it, or let ETS() estimate it, and the same data then gives four different futures.

    trend("Ad", phi = 0.9) # 1. fixed trend("Ad", phi = NULL, phi_range = c(0.8, 0.95)) # 2. estimated within bounds: lands on 0.95 trend("Ad") # 3. estimated freely: 0.98
    Forecast forHoltφ = 0.9φ in [0.8, 0.95]φ estimated
    201825.024.924.925.0
    202728.326.827.427.8
    203230.127.328.329.2
    • A year out, all four agree. Damping matters at long horizons, which is where straight lines get into trouble.
    • The bounded fit lands on its upper bound, 0.95: the data wanted less damping than the bounds allowed. When a fitted parameter sits on a bound, it is worth asking why.
    • Default: let ETS() estimate φ. Fix or bound it only with a reason from outside the data, such as a market you know is saturating.

    The point forecasts differ by three million people in 2032. Which one to trust is Session 14's question, answered by cross-validation.
